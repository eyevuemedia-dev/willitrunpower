Can a car battery run a CPAP? Nights, drain limits and safer options

Yes for a night or two with the humidifier off, if you use your CPAP maker's DC cord and the engine is off. A humidifier-off AirSense 11 takes about 5.5 Ah per night from a 12 V battery; with the humidifier and heated tube it takes about 43 Ah, which is nearly the whole 44 Ah rating of a typical Group 35 starting battery. Keep a starting battery above about 75% charge if you need the car to start: that is roughly 2.0 nights humidifier-off and about 2 hours with the heated tube. For more than that, use a separate deep-cycle or LiFePO4 battery, a CPAP battery, or a power station.

Medical safety notice: Will It Run? gives general power estimates only, not medical advice. Power draw varies by model, settings, pressure, humidifier and altitude. Before relying on any battery, power station, inverter or generator for a medical device, confirm approved power sources with the device manufacturer and your doctor or equipment supplier, and test your setup before an emergency. Never change prescribed settings to extend battery life. If your therapy is life-sustaining, keep the backup your supplier provides, register with your utility's medical-needs program, and have an evacuation plan. Run generators outdoors only, at least 20 ft from windows and doors, because of carbon monoxide risk. In an emergency, call 911.

Short answer

  • Night cost: about 5.5 Ah humidifier-off, 15 Ah with the HumidAir, 43 Ah with the heated tube (AirSense 11 on ResMed's DC converter, 8 hours).
  • Ignore cold-cranking amps. CCA is a 30-second cranking test. Use amp-hours (Ah, 20-hour rate) or reserve capacity (minutes at 25 A) to judge a night of therapy.
  • Starting batteries: fine for an occasional humidifier-off night; poor for repeated deep discharge. Battery makers say thin-plate starting batteries fail early when cycled deeply.
  • Engine off, outdoors only if it idles. Philips says not to run its machines from a vehicle battery while the engine is running. Never idle a car in a garage, even with the door open (CDC).
  • Use the maker's DC converter or cord, not a cheap inverter: an inverter's own ~15 W draw can be twice what a humidifier-off CPAP uses.
  • Better buys: a 100 Ah LiFePO4 battery (about 19 humidifier-off nights on paper), a 95 Wh CPAP battery for flights and one-night trips, or a 1 kWh power station with a 12 V port.

Read the battery label: Ah and reserve capacity, not CCA

A car battery label usually shows three numbers. Only two help with a CPAP.

  • CCA (cold-cranking amps): how hard the battery can crank an engine at 0°F. East Penn describes the CCA test as cranking over a 30-second period (East Penn). It says nothing about a night.
  • Ah (amp-hours, usually the 20-hour or C20 rate): the capacity. Multiply by 12 V for watt-hours. Optima's REDTOP 35 starting battery lists 44 Ah (Optima), which is 528 Wh on paper.
  • RC (reserve capacity, minutes): how long the battery holds 25 A before falling to 10.5 V at 80°F (Optima D34M spec sheet). If your label has no Ah figure, RC × 25 ÷ 60 gives a conservative amp-hour figure at that high current; a CPAP draws far less, so the C20 figure is closer for overnight use.

What one night costs in amp-hours

Your CPAP runs on 24 V inside. ResMed's DC/DC converters turn 12 V into 24 V; we allow 15% for that conversion and the cable. Amp-hours from the battery per 8-hour night = watts × 8 ÷ 0.85 ÷ 12 V. Watts come from ResMed's published power table, measured at typical settings (ResMed).

Higher pressure, a mask leak, a cold room or high humidity settings raise the draw. Philips does not publish DC draw for the DreamStation, so it is not modeled here.

Through a cheap 12 V inverter and the CPAP's own AC brick, add the inverter's idle. Using the ~15 W allowance this site uses for power-station inverters (BLUETTI's published self-consumption for its AC180; an owner test measured about 17.5 W on a DELTA 3 Plus), a humidifier-off night becomes about 17 Ah instead of 5.5. Plug-in inverters vary; a larger one can idle higher.

How far you can drain each battery

No single published "safe" depth fits every starting battery, so the numbers below are planning lines, labeled as ours where they are ours.

  • Starting battery you need tomorrow: stay above about 75%. Battery makers' test guides treat anything under 75% state of charge as needing a recharge before a load test (East Penn/Deka AGM guide, Crown Battery). We use that as the line for "the car still starts", not as a maker's CPAP rule. Cold mornings make it worse: CCA is rated at 0°F because cold batteries crank weaker.
  • Starting battery in an emergency: 50% at most, and expect wear. East Penn's marine guide says the thinner plates of a starting battery cannot handle constant deep cycling and will fail in short order (East Penn marine guide).
  • Dual-purpose or deep-cycle lead-acid: about 50%. That is the usual lead-acid planning figure; deeper drains shorten life.
  • LiFePO4: up to 100% per Battle Born, with its own BMS cutting off at 10 V (BB10012 data sheet). Leave a margin anyway for cold and aging.

Check it with a voltmeter, at rest. Open-circuit voltage after the battery has rested (makers say up to 24 hours for a true reading) tells you roughly where you are:

In the car: engine, accessory socket and carbon monoxide

  • Engine off. Philips' DreamStation 2 and DreamStation BiPAP manuals say not to run the device from a vehicle battery while the engine is running, because damage to the device may occur, and to use only a Philips Respironics DC cord (DreamStation 2, DreamStation BiPAP). ResMed's Air10 converter guide recommends a deep-cycle battery over a starting battery, says the converter is for negative-ground 12 V or 24 V systems, and says that if it cuts out when a vehicle starts, unplug it, wait and reconnect (Air10 DC/DC converter guide).
  • Never idle the car to "top up" in a garage. CDC: never run a car or truck inside a garage attached to your house, even with the garage door open (CDC). Sleeping in a closed car with the engine idling carries the same carbon monoxide risk; if you must recharge by running the engine, do it awake, outdoors, with the CPAP unplugged.
  • Check the socket. Many vehicles switch the 12 V accessory socket off with the ignition, or after a timeout. Try it on a weeknight in the driveway, not at the campsite. Some cars keep the socket live but have a battery-saver that kills it at a set voltage; that protects the starter battery but can end your therapy at 3 a.m.
  • Out-of-car battery wiring. For a loose deep-cycle battery, use a fused clamp-to-12 V-socket adapter or a battery box with a fused socket, and put the CPAP converter in that socket. Keep the converter uncovered while it runs (ResMed). Don't build a direct-wired lead without a fuse.
  • Start/stop and hybrid cars: many use AGM or EFB starting batteries and battery monitoring that may not like overnight drains. The owner's manual is the authority here; if in doubt, use a separate battery.

Recharging after a night

Put back what you used plus losses. East Penn's AGM guide says charging is never 100% efficient, so add 8–15% beyond what was removed, and a full charge takes about 3.5 hours longer than the simple math because the charger tapers near full (East Penn/Deka). Its table puts a 10 A charger at about 5.1 hours from 50% to full on a large truck battery. For the 44 Ah example, a humidifier-off night (5.5 Ah) needs about 6.3 Ah back; a heated-tube night (43 Ah) needs about 50 Ah.

A short drive does not reliably replace that: the alternator also has to run the car, and the last 10–20% of a lead-acid charge goes in slowly no matter the source. A plug-in smart charger (from shore power or a generator) or solar charging into a deep-cycle battery is the dependable way to recover a deep drain. Test it before you rely on it

  1. Fully charge the battery and let it rest; note the open-circuit voltage (or the lithium monitor percentage).
  2. Use your CPAP maker's DC converter or DC cord. For a loose battery, use a fused socket adapter.
  3. Run a full night at home, engine off, with your usual settings. Don't change prescribed pressure or humidity to stretch the battery; ask your clinician first.
  4. Next morning, let the battery rest and read the voltage again. Compare with the state-of-charge table and work out nights available.
  5. If it is the car's battery, start the car that morning to prove it still cranks, then recharge it fully.
  6. Write down what worked (battery, settings, voltage before and after) and keep the note with your kit.

What to buy

  • One night in the car, humidifier off: the car's battery and your maker's DC converter will usually do it. Check the socket stays live, keep above about 75%, and start the car in the morning before you pack up.
  • Flights and short trips: a 95–99 Wh CPAP battery matched to your machine, with humidity off.
  • Several nights camping or in a van: a 100 Ah LiFePO4 battery in a fused socket box, or a 1 kWh power station with a 12 V port. The station is simpler and also covers phones and lights; see CPAP camping.
  • Home outages: skip the car. Use a power station on the bedside AC outlet with UPS switchover, then move the CPAP to DC once the outage is clearly long. See CPAP during a power outage and the best power station for a CPAP.
  • Heated tube every night: plan on about 43 Ah per night at 12 V, which rules out the starting battery. Size for 2 kWh or more for three nights.

Questions

Can I run my CPAP from my car battery?

Yes, for a night or two with the humidifier off, using your CPAP maker's DC converter or cord and with the engine off. A humidifier-off AirSense 11 takes about 5.5 Ah per 8-hour night from a 12 V battery. If you need the car to start, keep the battery above about 75% charge, which is about two humidifier-off nights on a 44 Ah starting battery.

How many amp-hours does a CPAP use per night?

About 5.5 Ah at 12 V for a ResMed AirSense 11 with the humidifier off, about 15 Ah with the HumidAir humidifier and about 43 Ah with the humidifier and heated tube, based on ResMed's published power figures and a 15% DC converter allowance over 8 hours.

Will running a CPAP overnight drain my car battery so it won't start?

With the humidifier off, one night uses roughly an eighth of a 44 Ah starting battery, so the car will usually start. With a heated tube, one night can use nearly the whole rating and the car may not start. Measure the resting voltage in the morning; about 12.4 V flooded or 12.6 V AGM is roughly 75% charge.

Can I idle the car to keep the CPAP running?

No. Philips says not to run its CPAPs from a vehicle battery while the engine is running because the device may be damaged, and idling while asleep risks carbon monoxide poisoning. The CDC says never run a vehicle in an attached garage, even with the door open.

Is a deep-cycle or lithium battery better than a car battery for a CPAP?

Yes. A dual-purpose or deep-cycle lead-acid battery tolerates about 50% drains, and Battle Born rates its 100 Ah LiFePO4 battery for 100% depth of discharge, about 19 humidifier-off nights on paper. A starting battery's thin plates are not built for repeated deep cycling.

Should I use an inverter to run my CPAP from a car battery?

Use the CPAP maker's DC converter or cord if one exists. A plug-in inverter adds its own idle draw, often around 15 W, which is twice what a humidifier-off CPAP uses, so a 7 W night costs about 17 Ah instead of 5.5 Ah.
