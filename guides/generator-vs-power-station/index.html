Generator vs power station

A generator wins on energy: one gallon of gas delivers about 2.8–5.4 kWh in our 12-model set, more than a full charge of a 2 kWh power station, and it handles 240 V and big motor loads. A power station wins on safety and small loads: it runs indoors, silently, with zero fuel, and it is far more efficient for a CPAP, router or fridge overnight. For most homes the best answer is both: a 1–2 kWh station for nights and indoor loads, recharged by a small inverter generator for a couple of hours a day.

Medical safety notice: Will It Run? gives general power estimates only, not medical advice. Power draw varies by model, settings, pressure, humidifier and altitude. Before relying on any battery, power station, inverter or generator for a medical device, confirm approved power sources with the device manufacturer and your doctor or equipment supplier, and test your setup before an emergency. Never change prescribed settings to extend battery life. If your therapy is life-sustaining, keep the backup your supplier provides, register with your utility's medical-needs program, and have an evacuation plan. Run generators outdoors only, at least 20 ft from windows and doors, because of carbon monoxide risk. In an emergency, call 911.

Short answer

  • Apartment, condo, or no safe outdoor spot 20 ft from the house: power station. A generator is not an option without that space (CDC).
  • CPAP, router, phones, lights: power station. These loads are 7–60 W; a generator idling all night for them burns far more fuel than they use.
  • A fridge through a one-day outage: a 2 kWh station (see our 24-hour plan).
  • Three days or more, with fridge, freezer and sump: a generator, ideally paired with a station. Our 3-day plan shows the hybrid using about 1.4 gal over 72 hours versus about 8.4 gal running a generator around the clock.
  • 240 V well pump, whole-house inlet, central AC: a generator with an L14-30R, or one of the few 240 V stations (EcoFlow DELTA Pro 3, Anker SOLIX F3800), which cost $2,799 and up at Nov 19 maker prices.
  • Running cost: gasoline at $3.64/gal works out to about $0.67–$1.29 per kWh at 25% load; recharging a station from the grid at the U.S. average 18.17¢/kWh costs about $0.21 per kWh delivered.

Energy: one gallon vs one charge

The core difference is where the energy lives. A power station carries a fixed amount: a 2,048 Wh EcoFlow DELTA 2 Max gives about 1.74 kWh at our 85% allowance, then needs a wall, a car, solar or a generator. A generator carries its energy as fuel, and gasoline is dense. On running cost alone, a generator's electricity costs roughly three to six times grid power. That rarely matters for a once-a-year outage. It matters a lot if you plan to run off-grid for weeks, or if your "outage plan" is really an RV or cabin plan, where solar plus battery pays off fastest.

The light-load problem: why generators waste fuel overnight

Overnight loads are small. Our 24-hour outage plan's first tier (router, two LED bulbs, phones and a humidifier-off CPAP) is about 745 Wh for the whole day, a 31 W average. A generator can't throttle down to 31 W. Honda's lowest published point for the EU2200i is 25% load, 8.1 hours per 0.95 gal, which is about 0.117 gal an hour. Its Eco Throttle should burn less below that, but Honda doesn't publish a lighter figure, so the honest bound is: if it burned at the quarter-load rate, 24 hours would be about 2.8 gal ($10.25) to deliver 0.745 kWh.

The same day from a power station is one charge of a 1 kWh unit, silent, indoors, with nothing to refill until morning. This is why every CPAP and medical-backup guide on this site starts with a battery. If you need a CPAP through an outage, see the best power station for a CPAP and DC vs AC for the extra nights you get from a DC cord.

Noise, indoor use and upkeep

  • Noise: the generators in our database list 48 dB(A) (Honda EU2200i at 1/4 load) to 74 dBA (Westinghouse WGEN9500DF). Power stations have only cooling fans and the makers we track don't publish a dBA figure, so we don't print one. Our quietest inverter generators guide estimates each generator at 50 ft.
  • Indoors: a power station can sit in the bedroom or kitchen. A generator never runs inside, in a garage or near openings; CDC guidance is outdoors only, at least 20 feet from doors, windows and vents (CDC). Several of the generators here have CO shutoff sensors; they reduce risk but do not make indoor use safe.
  • Upkeep: a generator needs oil changes, fresh or stabilized fuel, and a monthly run so the carburetor doesn't gum up. A station needs to be kept charged per the maker's storage instructions and, ideally, run through a cycle a few times a year.
  • Ready time: a station is on the instant the power drops, and some act as a UPS (EcoFlow's DELTA 3 Plus lists under 10 ms). A generator has to be wheeled out, fueled, started and cabled in, usually in bad weather.

The hybrid: run the generator hard, briefly

A generator is most efficient near its rating; a station is most efficient at small loads. Combine them and each does what it's good at. With Honda's EU2200i figures, recharging a 2 kWh station at about 1,800 W takes about 1.6 hours a day and 0.47 gal, versus 2.8 gal a day running the generator around the clock at quarter load. Over three days that's 1.4 gal instead of 8.4 gal, and the nights are silent. Check that the station's AC charge rate can be turned down to what the generator supplies; the full math is in our 3-day outage plan.

Safety first

  • Run a generator outdoors only, at least 20 feet from the house, with the exhaust pointed away from doors, windows and vents. Never in a garage, basement, shed or crawlspace, even with the door open.
  • Carbon monoxide is odorless and can kill in minutes. Keep battery CO alarms working inside the home.
  • Never backfeed a house by plugging a generator into a wall outlet or dryer receptacle. It can energize utility lines and kill a lineworker or a neighbor.
  • Connecting to home wiring requires a transfer switch or a listed breaker interlock with a power inlet box, installed by a licensed electrician and permitted where your jurisdiction requires it.

Questions

Is a generator or a power station better for a power outage?

For short outages and indoor or medical loads, a power station: it is silent, safe indoors and efficient for small loads. For multi-day outages, 240 V loads or a whole-house inlet, a generator. For most homes the best plan is both: a station for nights, recharged by a small generator for an hour or two a day.

How much energy does a gallon of gas make in a generator?

About 2.8 to 5.4 kWh per gallon at 25% load across the 12 gasoline generators we calculated from maker runtimes, and more near full load (about 6.1 kWh per gallon for a Honda EU2200i at 1,800 W). That is more than a full charge of a 2 kWh power station.

Is it cheaper to run a generator or a power station?

Per kWh, a station recharged from the grid is cheaper: about $0.21 per kWh at the U.S. average residential price of 18.17¢, versus about $0.67 to $1.29 per kWh of gasoline at $3.64 a gallon and 25% load. Solar recharging has no fuel cost at all.

Can a power station replace a generator?

For small loads and short outages, yes. For 240 V well pumps, central air or multi-day whole-house backup, only the largest stations (4 kWh class, 113 lb and up) come close, and they still need recharging. A generator remains the practical choice there.

Can I run a generator overnight for a CPAP?

It is a poor fit. A CPAP uses 7 to 60 W, the generator must run outdoors at least 20 ft from the house, and it burns fuel at close to its lowest-load rate all night. A power station by the bed, ideally on a DC cord, is safer and far more efficient. Confirm power options with your equipment supplier.

Can I use a generator to recharge a power station?

Yes, and it is the most fuel-efficient way to use a small generator. Set the station's AC charge rate at or below the generator's running watts, charge for an hour or two at high load, then shut the generator off and run the house on the battery.
