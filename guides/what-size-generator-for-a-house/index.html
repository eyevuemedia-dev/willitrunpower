What size generator do you need to run a house?

Add up the running watts of everything you need at once, then add the single biggest starting surge. On Honda and Champion chart figures, a gas-heat, city-water house (fridge, freezer, furnace blower, sump pump, lights, internet, microwave) runs at about 3,544 W and needs about 5,694 W at the worst start, so a 5,500–7,500 W generator covers it. A 240 V well pump pushes that to 6,694–9,544 W and needs a 120/240 V outlet and an inlet. Central air or an all-electric house is standby-generator territory: Generac puts most 2,000 sq ft homes at 18–22 kW.

Safety first: run a generator outdoors only, at least 20 feet from the house, exhaust pointed away from doors, windows and vents, and keep battery CO alarms working inside. Never backfeed through a wall or dryer outlet; hard-wired circuits need a transfer switch or a listed interlock with an inlet, installed by a licensed electrician (CDC). If anyone depends on powered medical equipment, plan that load first: see CPAP during a power outage.

Step 1: list the loads, with running and starting watts

Every motor has two numbers. Running watts are what it draws once it is turning. Starting watts are the one- or two-second burst to get the compressor or pump moving, often two to three times the running figure. Resistive loads like lights, a microwave or a water heater start at their running watts. Use the nameplate on your own appliances when you can; these are the manufacturer chart figures we used:

Step 2: do the math (worked example)

Take the gas-heat, city-water essentials list: fridge, freezer, furnace blower, sump pump, ten LED bulbs, internet, a TV, chargers and a microwave. Running watts add up to 3,544 W. The biggest starting number is the 1/2 hp sump pump at 2,150 W. Generac's formula adds them: 3,544 + 2,150 = 5,694 W. If the pump kicks on while everything else is already running, the real surge is a bit lower, 3,544 − 1,050 + 2,150 = 4,644 W, but you don't control when a sump float or a fridge compressor starts.

So you want a generator with at least 3,544 running watts and about 5,694 starting watts. Leave the microwave for when the pumps and furnace are idle and you drop to 2,544 W running and 4,694 W at the worst start, which is how a mid-size inverter generator gets through a night. Remember that running a generator near its rated watts for hours cuts fuel runtime and raises noise; Honda's own runtime figures are quoted at rated load and at 1/4 load for that reason.

Step 3: check voltage and how it connects

  • Plug-in loads only. A fridge, lamps, router and TV can run on heavy extension cords from any generator. A furnace, sump pump on a dedicated circuit or a well pump is hard-wired, so it needs a transfer switch or an interlock and inlet.
  • 240 V loads. Most deep-well pumps, central AC, electric water heaters, dryers and ranges are 240 V. They need a generator with a 120/240 V outlet (L14-30R or 14-50R) feeding an inlet. A 120 V L5-30R or the RV-style TT-30R can't run them.
  • Inlet limits. A 30 A, 120/240 V inlet carries at most 30 × 240 = 7,200 W; a 50 A inlet, 50 × 240 = 12,000 W. A bigger generator behind a 30 A inlet only helps with starting surges. Details: whole house via a 30 amp inlet.
  • Clean power. Inverter generators typically publish under 3% total harmonic distortion, which is what you want for electronics, furnace control boards and medical devices. Open-frame models are cheaper per watt but often don't publish THD.

Well pumps need more generator than their watts suggest

A submersible pump draws a hard surge, and an undersized generator lets the voltage sag until the motor stalls. Franklin Electric's AIM manual lists the smallest generator for the pump on its own (continuous-duty, externally regulated; internally regulated generators can be smaller):

Franklin also says to start the generator before the pump and shut the pump off before the generator, because a coasting generator can damage the motor's thrust bearing. That is the pump alone; add the running watts of everything else that is on when it starts.

Central air is what turns a portable job into a standby job

Champion's chart puts a 3-ton central AC at 3,000–3,500 W running and 5,000–6,000 W starting. Generac is blunter: 3.5–5 kW running, and up to 15–18 kW to start the compressor without help. That gap is why the compressor nameplate matters: look for LRA (locked-rotor amps). LRA × 240 V is roughly the worst-case starting load. A soft-start kit cuts that surge, and Generac's answer for a 3-ton unit is a 14–18 kW standby generator with a load manager or soft start rather than a bigger generator. Add the AC to the essentials and you are at 8,044 W running before any start, already more than a 30 A inlet can carry.

Go all-electric (water heater, dryer, range) and the chart total reaches 20,044 W running, which lines up with Generac's 20–22 kW recommendation for whole-home coverage of a 2,000 sq ft house. Generac also stresses that square footage is only a starting point; two identical floor plans can differ by 8 kW depending on heat source, well and AC.

Generator or batteries?

For a fridge, CPAP, router and lights through an overnight outage, a portable power station is quieter and needs no fuel or extension-cord run outside. For multi-day outages, a well pump, or anything 240 V, a generator wins. Many households pair them: run the generator in the day, the battery at night. See generator vs power station, the fridge outage guide and best power station for a sump pump.

Questions

What size generator do I need to run a whole house?

For true whole-house coverage including central AC and electric appliances, a home standby generator in the 18–22 kW range is typical; Generac calls 22 kW the industry standard for whole-home protection on 2,500–3,000 sq ft houses. On chart figures, an all-electric house with a 3-ton AC, well pump, water heater, dryer and range adds up to about 20,044 W running. A portable generator through a 30 A inlet can deliver at most 7,200 W, so it covers essential circuits, not the whole house.

Can a 7,500 watt generator run a house?

It can run the essentials. Our gas-heat, city-water essentials list is about 3,544 W running and 5,694 W at the worst start, and with a 1/2 hp well pump it is 4,544 W running and 6,694 W at start, inside a 7,500 W generator with 9,000+ W starting. It will not run central air, an electric water heater and a dryer together.

Can a 3,500 watt generator run a house?

Only a slice of it. Fridge, freezer, furnace blower, lights, internet and TV come to about 1,494 W running, which fits, but the sump pump and microwave push the total past 3,500 W. Start one motor at a time and skip 240 V loads; a 3,500 W unit has no 240 V outlet.

What size generator do I need for a 2,000 square foot house?

Square footage is only a starting point. Generac says a 2,000 sq ft house typically needs 20–22 kW for whole-home coverage, but the same house with gas heat, city water and no central AC can get through an outage on essential circuits with a 5,500–7,500 W portable generator. List your loads instead of using floor area.

What size generator do I need for a well pump?

Franklin Electric's AIM manual lists the minimum generator for the pump alone: 2 kW for a 1/2 hp 3-wire motor and 4 kW for 1 hp (externally regulated generators), and 50% more for 2-wire motors (3 kW and 6 kW). Add everything else that runs while the pump starts. Most deep-well pumps are 240 V, so the generator needs a 120/240 V outlet.

Do I need a transfer switch?

To power anything hard-wired (a furnace, well pump, sump pump on a circuit, central AC), yes: a transfer switch or a listed interlock with a power inlet box, installed by a licensed electrician. Never backfeed through a dryer or wall outlet. Extension cords are fine only for plug-in appliances.
