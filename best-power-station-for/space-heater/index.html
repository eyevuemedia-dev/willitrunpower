Best power station for a space heater: the 1,500 W limit

Most portable electric heaters in the U.S. top out at 1,500 W because that is about 12.5 A on a 120 V household circuit. A power station can run one, but only if its published continuous AC rating covers the setting you actually use, and only for a short time. Using our standard rule (watt-hours × 0.85 ÷ (watts + about 15 W of inverter draw)), the Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 (1,070 Wh, 1,500 W continuous) runs a 1,500 W heater about 36 minutes. A 2 kWh station such as the EcoFlow DELTA 2 Max or Jackery Explorer 2000 v2 lasts about 1.1 hours. Even the EcoFlow DELTA Pro 3 (4,096 Wh) manages about 2.3 hours on high. Eight hours at 1,500 W would take about 14,259 Wh of nameplate capacity, which is not a portable-station job. If the goal is staying warm through an outage, heat the person, not the room: Sunbeam lists its 60×80 heated throw (model 32810124) at 100 W, and the same 1,070 Wh station then lasts about 7.9 hours. Use a 1,500 W heater on a station only to take the chill off a closed room for less than an hour, and only on a unit whose continuous rating — not its surge or a heater-only boost mode — covers 1,500 W.

What portable heaters actually draw

Almost every portable electric heater sold for U.S. homes is a 1,500 W appliance. That is not a coincidence. A standard 15 A, 120 V receptacle can supply 1,800 W; 1,500 W is 12.5 A, which leaves a little room on the circuit and matches what heater manuals warn about. Honeywell's HCE317 series manual says outright, "This heater draws 12.5 amps," and lists a maximum of 1,500 W at 120 V. Lasko's official page for the 755320 (the live listing now 404s; this is the archived copy) calls it a 1,500 W digital ceramic heater with two heat settings, an 8-hour timer, oscillation, overheat protection and a cool-touch exterior.

Watts below are manufacturer nameplate or manual figures, not measurements from our bench. A resistive element does not have a separate motor surge; when the element is on, it draws the setting. Thermostat cycling can lower the average in a room that has already reached setpoint — we do not assume that in a cold outage.

Two things follow. First, "a space heater" is not one load: the same household box can be 1,500 W, 900 W, 750 W or 700 W depending on the model and the switch. Second, ceramic fan heaters and oil-filled radiators use about the same watts while their elements are on. The difference is what happens when the thermostat opens. A ceramic heater with a fan stops making useful heat almost immediately. An oil-filled radiator, as DeLonghi notes of the type, keeps giving off stored heat after the element cycles. That can lower average watts in a closed room that has already warmed up. It does not change the continuous rating you need for the setting you selected, and we do not invent a duty cycle for a cold house with no central heat.

Continuous watts, not surge or boost

This is the rule that decides the rest of the page: only recommend a station whose published AC continuous rating covers the heater setting. For high, that means at least 1,500 W continuous. Peak, surge and "boost" numbers are the usual sources of bad advice on this topic.

  • Surge or peak is a short overload, typically a second or two, so a fridge or pump motor can start. A heater is not a motor. It stays at nameplate watts until the thermostat or a safety switch opens.
  • Resistive boost modes — EcoFlow X-Boost, BLUETTI Power Lifting, Anker SurgePad — are designed for heaters and kettles. BLUETTI's AC180 page says Power Lifting can run heating devices up to 2,700 W and, in the FAQ, "Do NOT use this unit to run an air conditioner or washing machine." Those modes often lower the output voltage. They are not the same as a 1,500 W continuous rating, and we do not use them to clear a station on this guide.
  • Our individual appliance tables are more permissive. The Lasko 755320 high page marks some sub-1,500 W stations as "Runs" because those rows treat a documented resistive boost as eligible. This guide does not. If you want a heater to run as a sustained load, use the continuous number.
  • Exactly 1,500 W continuous is the floor, not comfortable headroom. The Jackery Explorer 1000 v2, Jackery HomePower 1000 v2 and Goal Zero Yeti 1000X are all rated 1,500 W continuous. They can cover high, but you should not plug anything else into the AC outlets at the same time. Some inverters also derate in a hot room: the AC180 spec table limits discharge to 1,500 W between 86 °F and 104 °F. Keep the station out of the heater's airstream.

Stations in our set that do not clear 1,500 W continuous: EcoFlow RIVER 2 (300 W), Anker SOLIX C300 (300 W), Goal Zero Yeti 500X (300 W), EcoFlow RIVER 2 Max (500 W), EcoFlow RIVER 3 Plus (600 W), EcoFlow RIVER 2 Pro (800 W), BLUETTI AC70 (1,000 W), Anker SOLIX C800 (1,200 W). The Anker SOLIX C800 at 1,200 W and the BLUETTI AC70 at 1,000 W are the ones people most often expect to run a heater because 1,500 W appears somewhere on the spec sheet as a surge. It is not enough.

How long a charge actually lasts

Every hour on this page uses the same published rule as our methodology and the rest of the buying guides: hours = nameplate Wh × 0.85 ÷ (load W + about 15 W). The 0.85 is our allowance for inverter loss, not a measured efficiency for any one model. The 15 W is inverter self-consumption; BLUETTI publishes about 15 W in the AC180 runtime formula. On a 1,500 W heater that extra 15 W barely moves the answer (about 35.7 minutes vs 36.0 from a 1,070 Wh pack if you omit it). On a 100 W throw it matters a lot.

Better ways to stay warm on a battery

If you already own a 1,500 W heater and a station that can run it, using high for 30–70 minutes to take the edge off a closed room is reasonable. Building a kit around that load is usually not. These alternatives cut watts, which is the only way a portable battery becomes an overnight heat plan.

Use the low setting you actually have

On a Lasko 755320, that is the 900 W end of the published range. On a Honeywell HCE317 series heater, the manual's low setting is 750 W — half of high. On a DeLonghi 1,500 W oil-filled radiator, the official minimum is 700 W. Any of those moves a 1 kWh station from roughly 35 minutes toward an hour or a bit more, and they let smaller inverters onto the list. The Anker SOLIX C800 (1,200 W continuous) and BLUETTI AC70 (1,000 W) clear 900 W and 750 W; the EcoFlow RIVER 2 Pro (800 W) clears 750 W but not 900 W. The EcoFlow RIVER 3 Plus at 600 W continuous does not clear 750 W without a boost mode we are not counting.

Oil-filled versus ceramic

NFPA's portable heater tip sheet lists both types: ceramic heaters warm air over a ceramic element with a fan; oil- or water-filled radiators circulate heated fluid and radiate. Watts while the element is on are similar. Oil-filled units are slower to warm a room, quieter (no fan), and keep radiating after the thermostat opens, which is why they sometimes use fewer watt-hours in a closed room that has already reached temperature. In a leaking, unheated house during an outage, plan as if the element will stay on. Place either type on a solid, flat floor, not a rug, which is also an NFPA item.

Heat the person: a listed heated throw

Sunbeam's 60×80 microplush heated throw (model 32810124) is specified at 100 W, with four heat settings and a 4-hour auto-off. That is the load that makes a 1 kWh station look like an overnight device (7.9 hours on the Jackery Explorer 1000 v2; 15.1 hours on the EcoFlow DELTA 2 Max). Even the EcoFlow RIVER 2 (256 Wh, 300 W continuous) is estimated at about 1.9 hr — short of a full night, but in a different league from a heater. A 4-hour auto-off means you may need to switch it back on; budget a second cycle if you plan to sleep longer than that, and follow the textile's own instructions. We are not recommending anyone disable a safety timer.

Dress in layers and close off rooms

These do not have nameplate watts, which is the point. Close doors to rooms you are not using. Hang a blanket over a doorway if the door is leaky. Put on a hat, wool socks and an extra sweater before you spend 1,500 W to heat air that will leak out of a 1,500-square-foot house. A closed 150-square-foot room — the kind of small-room figure Home Depot lists for the Lasko 755320 — is the only space a portable heater plus a portable battery can plausibly condition, and even then only briefly. Do not heat yourself with a gas oven; the CDC says never to heat a house that way.

Fire, tip-over, circuits and carbon monoxide

Affiliate relationships do not change any of the following. They come from safety agencies and from the manuals, not from anyone who sells a power station.

  • Clearance. CPSC and NFPA both say to keep a portable heater at least 3 feet (1 metre) from anything that can burn, including bedding, furniture, drapes, papers — and people, in NFPA's wording. That 3-foot bubble should also include the power station. Hot exhaust on a lithium battery is a bad combination, and several stations derate or shut down when they get warm.
  • Tip-over and overheat protection. NFPA says to choose a heater with a thermostat, overheat protection, and an auto shut-off if it tips over, and to place it on a solid, flat surface. Lasko's 755320 page lists overheat protection and a cool-touch exterior; it does not mention a tip-over switch. Consumer Reports' listing for the 755320 marks "Tip-over safety switch" as no. Honeywell's HCE317 series manual describes a 360° tip-over switch that disables the heater if it is tipped in any direction. If you are buying a heater to keep with a power station, prefer one whose maker documents tip-over shutoff.
  • Never leave it running unattended, and never run it while you sleep. CPSC: never leave portable heaters on while sleeping, and do not leave them running unattended in a confined space around infants or people who may not be able to react to rising heat (CPSC also flags a hyperthermia hazard). NFPA: turn the heater off and unplug it when you leave the room or go to bed. A power station with UPS or EPS mode does not make an unattended heater a good idea.
  • No extension cords or power strips. CPSC and NFPA both say to plug the heater directly into a wall outlet. Do the same at the station. CPSC estimated about 1,700 portable-heater fires a year from 2017 to 2019, with about 70 deaths and 160 injuries. NFPA notes that two in five deaths in space-heater fires involve portable electric heaters.
  • One heater, one outlet, watch the circuit. A 1,500 W heater is already 12.5 A. On household power, do not share that receptacle with other loads, and stop if the plug, cord or faceplate is hot (a CPSC check). On a station, do not pile a kettle, microwave or another heater onto the same inverter. The continuous rating is a ceiling, not a suggestion.
  • Electric heaters do not make carbon monoxide; generators and fuel heaters do. CDC and CPSC both say to run a portable generator outdoors only, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents, never in a home, garage, basement or shed, even with doors open. CDC also says never to heat a house with a gas oven, never to burn charcoal indoors, and never to use a portable gas camp stove or portable flameless chemical heater indoors. If a long outage tempts you toward a generator so you can keep running a heater, put the generator where the CDC says and use a battery-backup CO alarm.
  • Test it before you need it. Plug the heater into the station on a day when the grid is up, set the station where it will actually sit (3 feet from the heater, dry, upright), turn the heater to the setting you plan to use, and watch the station's watt reading and any over-temperature warning for 15 minutes. If the inverter faults, the watt reading is above the continuous rating, or the station gets hot, that combination is not your outage plan.

Questions

Can a portable power station run a 1,500 W space heater?

Yes, if the station's published continuous AC output is at least 1,500 W. That includes the Jackery Explorer 1000 v2, Jackery HomePower 1000 v2 and Goal Zero Yeti 1000X at exactly 1,500 W, and higher-output units such as the BLUETTI AC180 (1,800 W), Jackery Explorer 2000 v2 (2,200 W) and EcoFlow DELTA 2 Max (2,400 W). A 1,500 W surge or peak figure is not enough, because a resistive heater draws that load the whole time the element is on. Stations under 1,500 W continuous — including the BLUETTI AC70 (1,000 W), Anker SOLIX C800 (1,200 W) and EcoFlow RIVER 2 Pro (800 W) — are not recommended on high, even if a boost mode claims to run heaters.

How long will a 1 kWh power station run a space heater?

About 36 minutes on a 1,500 W setting, or about 60 min on a 900 W low setting and about 1.2 hr on a documented 750 W setting, using watt-hours × 0.85 ÷ (watts + 15 W). The Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 is 1,070 Wh; the Jackery HomePower 1000 v2 is 1,024 Wh and lasts about 34 minutes on high. These are estimates, not measurements. Thermostat cycling in a small, already-warm room can stretch them; a cold house during an outage often will not.

Why isn't a 1,500 W surge rating enough for a heater?

Surge and peak ratings describe a brief overload, usually meant for a motor starting. A space heater is a resistive load: it pulls its nameplate watts continuously until the thermostat opens. If the station's continuous rating is 1,000 W and its surge is 1,500 W, the inverter is not specified to hold 1,500 W. Treat the continuous number as the one that matters, and do not add other AC loads on top of the heater.

What size power station do I need to run a heater overnight?

For eight hours of continuous 1,500 W heat you would need about 14,259 Wh of nameplate capacity. At 900 W that is about 8,612 Wh, and at 750 W about 7,200 Wh. No portable station in our set reaches those figures. Eight hours of a 100 W heated throw needs about 1,082 Wh, which a 1 kWh LiFePO4 station covers with margin.

Is the low or 750 W setting a better use of the battery?

Yes, if the heater actually has that setting and the station's continuous rating covers it. <a href="https://m.media-amazon.com/images/I/B1qi8lVajEL.pdf" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Honeywell's HCE317 series manual</a> lists low at 750 W and high at 1,500 W (other Honeywell series in the same manual use 900 W on low). On the Jackery Explorer 1000 v2, 750 W lasts about 1.2 hr versus 36 minutes on high. The EcoFlow RIVER 2 Pro (800 W continuous) can cover a 750 W setting but not 900 W or 1,500 W. Lower watts also leave inverter headroom so you can run a lamp or phone charger at the same time.

Are heated blankets a better use of a power station than a space heater?

For staying warm, usually yes. <a href="https://sunbeamhome.com/products/sunbeam-heated-merlot-throw-blanket-oversized-microplush-4-heat-settings-60x80" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Sunbeam publishes 100 W</a> for its 60×80 microplush heated throw (model 32810124, four heat settings, 4-hour auto-off). That is 6.7% of a 1,500 W heater, so a 1 kWh station goes from roughly half an hour of room heat to about 7.9 hours of personal heat. Follow the blanket's own instructions, including its auto-off, and do not bury a controller or fold a heated textile into a tight ball. This is about energy, not a claim that any textile is safer than a listed heater.

Can I use an extension cord between the heater and the power station?

<a href="https://www.cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/2023/CPSC-Warns-Consumers-to-be-Cautious-When-Using-Space-Heaters-Furnaces-and-Fireplaces-This-Winter" rel="noopener" target="_blank">CPSC</a> and <a href="https://www.nfpa.org/-/media/project/storefront/catalog/files/safety-tip-sheets/portableheatersafetytips.pdf" rel="noopener" target="_blank">NFPA</a> both say to plug a portable heater directly into a wall outlet and never into an extension cord or power strip. Treat the station's AC outlet the same way: plug the heater straight into the station, and put the station close enough that you do not need a cord, but at least 3 feet from the heater so the exhaust does not cook the battery. Do not run the heater's cord under a rug.

Is it safe to run a space heater from a power station while I sleep?

CPSC says never leave a portable heater on while sleeping, and NFPA says to turn the heater off and unplug it when you leave the room or go to bed. That advice does not change because the outlet is on a battery. CPSC estimated that portable heaters were involved in about 1,700 fires, 70 deaths and 160 injuries a year from 2017 to 2019. Choose a heater with a tip-over shutoff and overheat protection, keep 3 feet of clearance, and never run it unattended. If you need warmth overnight, a listed heated throw or mattress pad with its own auto-off is the lower-watt option; still follow that product's instructions.
