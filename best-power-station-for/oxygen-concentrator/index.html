Can a power station run an oxygen concentrator? Runtimes from maker wattages

Yes, but for far less time than most people expect. A typical 5-liter home concentrator draws 275–350 W by its maker's own spec, so a 1 kWh station such as the Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 runs one for only about 2.5–3.1 hours. Treat a power station as a bridge that keeps oxygen flowing while you switch to the backup cylinders your supplier provides, not as your outage plan. Getting through an 8-hour night at 350 W takes about 3,435 Wh, which means a 4 kWh-class unit like the EcoFlow DELTA Pro 3. The exception is a low-draw unit at a low prescribed setting: the Inogen At Home at setting 2 is about 100 W, and a 2 kWh station covers that for roughly 15 hours.

Medical note. This page is general information about electricity, not medical advice. Your oxygen prescription, your doctor and your oxygen supplier decide your backup plan. Before an outage, ask your supplier for a backup supply that does not need electricity (cylinders) and learn how to switch to it, as the American Lung Association advises. Jackery says its power stations are not recommended for medical devices, and EcoFlow says its products are not intended for life-sustaining equipment. If you are short of breath or your oxygen is running out, call 911. Two things stand out. Power depends on the setting, so ask your supplier for the figure at your prescribed flow, or measure it with a plug-in watt meter for an hour. And most makers publish only an average or typical number, not the start-up surge of the compressor. The nameplate current is a useful ceiling: 3 A at 120 V is 360 VA for the Invacare and 3.3 A at 115 V is about 380 VA for the DeVilbiss. Every station on this page is rated for 1,500 W continuous or more, so steady output is not the problem; battery size is.

The Inogen At Home manual also lists 275 W max; Inogen's 100 W figure is at setting 2. Philips lists lower figures for 220–230 V models; this page uses the 120 V numbers.

At 300–350 W, 24 hours is roughly 9–10 kWh, the size of a home battery wall or several of the largest portable stations. That is why a battery alone is not a multi-day oxygen plan. Over a long outage, the options that scale are your supplier's cylinders, a generator running outdoors to recharge the station (see what size generator and generator vs power station), or going somewhere with power. Solar helps but is slow: a 400 W panel array on a good winter day harvests well under one night's energy for a 350 W concentrator. Our 3-day outage planner shows the full budget for a household.

Switchover and restart

When the wall power drops, a concentrator stops and sounds its power-failure alarm. A power station in pass-through or UPS mode can keep it running without a gap, which matters because some units take time to come back to full purity: Invacare says the Perfecto2 V reaches its rated concentration after about 30 minutes of warm-up, and Inogen says under 5 minutes for the At Home.

Test before you need it: with the concentrator running from the station and the station plugged into the wall, unplug the station and listen. No alarm and no restart means the switchover works for your unit. Also turn off any energy-saving or AC auto-off timer (Jackery ships the 1000 v2 with Energy Saving Mode on), and check that the station's fan noise and the concentrator's exhaust have room to breathe; Inogen asks for at least 6 inches of clearance and Invacare 12 inches.

Portable oxygen concentrators (POCs)

A POC is the easier load. It has its own battery (Inogen rates the One G5 at up to 6.5 hours on the single battery and 13 hours on the double, depending on setting) and can be charged from a power station. Two cautions from the Inogen One G5 manual:

  • DC voltage. The G5's DC cable expects 13.5–15.0 V at up to 10 A. The 12 V car ports on the stations we track are listed at 12 V, below that range, so plan on the AC power supply in an AC outlet unless your station maker publishes a car-port voltage inside it.
  • No published watts. Inogen lists the AC supply's input current (2.0–1.0 A), not average power. Measure your POC with a plug-in watt meter while it runs at your setting and charges a battery, then use the runtime formula above.

Spare charged POC batteries and a car charger are on the American Lung Association's outage checklist; they cost less than a big station and don't depend on an inverter.

A realistic outage plan

  1. Get a non-electric backup. Ask your oxygen supplier for cylinders sized to your prescription and how many hours each lasts. Learn to connect one before you need it.
  2. Register as power-dependent. Tell your electric utility, and local fire and police, that you rely on home oxygen; many utilities run a medical-priority program.
  3. Size the battery as a bridge. Pick a station that covers at least the time it takes to switch to cylinders or reach help, using the tables above with your unit's real draw.
  4. Plan the recharge. A generator outdoors, never in a garage or near a window, or a place with power. Carbon monoxide is a bigger risk than a dead battery.
  5. Don't turn the flow down to stretch the battery unless your doctor tells you to.
  6. Test twice a year: full charge, real unplug test, and a timed run so you know your actual hours.

Questions

How long will a power station run an oxygen concentrator?

Divide the station's watt-hours by the concentrator's watts, after allowing for losses. We use Wh × 0.85 ÷ (concentrator watts + 15 W inverter draw). A 1,070 Wh Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 runs a 350 W Philips EverFlo about 2.5 hours and an Inogen At Home at setting 2 (about 100 W) about 7.9 hours. A 4,096 Wh EcoFlow DELTA Pro 3 runs the same units about 9.5 and 30.3 hours.

What size power station do I need for an oxygen concentrator overnight?

For 8 hours, multiply (watts + 15) by 8 and divide by 0.85. That is about 3,435 Wh for a 350 W EverFlo, 3,200 Wh for a 325 W Invacare Perfecto2 V, 2,729 Wh for a concentrator at 275 W and 1,082 Wh for an Inogen At Home at setting 2. Use your own unit's spec at your prescribed flow, and add margin because batteries lose capacity in the cold and with age.

Can I run an oxygen concentrator from a 1,000 Wh power station?

Yes, for a few hours. Every 1 kWh-class station we list has enough continuous output (1,500 W or more), but at 275–350 W the battery is empty in about 2.5–3.1 hours. That is a useful bridge while you change over to cylinders or get to a place with power, not an overnight plan.

Will a power station run a portable oxygen concentrator (POC)?

Usually yes, through the POC's own AC power supply. Check the DC route first: the Inogen One G5 manual lists a DC input of 13.5–15.0 V, while the car ports on the stations we track are listed as 12 V. Use the maker's AC adapter in an AC outlet unless your station maker publishes a car-port voltage inside your POC's range. Inogen rates the G5 at up to 6.5 hours on a single battery and 13 hours on a double, depending on setting.

Should I lower my oxygen flow to make the battery last longer?

No, not without your doctor. The American Lung Association says not to reduce your oxygen flow rate to stretch a battery or oxygen supply without talking to your healthcare provider.

Is a power station safe for medical equipment?

Some makers say no. Jackery says its power stations are not recommended for medical devices, EcoFlow says its products are not intended for life-sustaining equipment, and Invacare describes the Perfecto2 V as not intended to sustain or support life. Ask your doctor and equipment supplier before you rely on any battery, and keep a non-electric backup oxygen supply.
