24-hour outage: what size power station gets you through one day

For lights, Wi-Fi, phones and a humidifier-off CPAP, a 1 kWh station covers 24 hours with room to spare (about 745 Wh). Add a full-size fridge and you need the 2 kWh class: a median top-freezer fridge brings the day to about 1,739 Wh, right at the 1,741 Wh a 2,048 Wh station delivers, and a bottom-freezer or French-door fridge (2,218 Wh) runs a 2 kWh station out after about 19 hours. Our pick for a one-day outage with a fridge is the Jackery Explorer 2000 v2 ($999, in stock at Jackery today); without a fridge, the Anker SOLIX C1000 ($499.99). A full chest or upright freezer can stay unplugged: FoodSafety.gov says a full freezer holds for about 48 hours with the door shut.

Medical safety notice: Will It Run? gives general power estimates only, not medical advice. Power draw varies by model, settings, pressure, humidifier and altitude. Before relying on any battery, power station, inverter or generator for a medical device, confirm approved power sources with the device manufacturer and your doctor or equipment supplier, and test your setup before an emergency. Never change prescribed settings to extend battery life. If your therapy is life-sustaining, keep the backup your supplier provides, register with your utility's medical-needs program, and have an evacuation plan. Run generators outdoors only, at least 20 ft from windows and doors, because of carbon monoxide risk. In an emergency, call 911.

Short answer

  • No fridge: about 745 Wh for the day. Any 1 kWh station (870 Wh usable) does it; a 768 Wh unit just about does.
  • Top-freezer fridge: about 1,739 Wh. A 2 kWh station, with nothing else big plugged in.
  • Bottom-freezer or French-door fridge: about 2,218 Wh. A 2 kWh station gets you about 19 hours, so plan one recharge or a cooler with ice for the last few hours.
  • Fridge, TV and a humidified CPAP: about 2,918 Wh. That is more than even a 3.84 kWh F3800 Plus delivers in a day once its 40 W idle is counted; plan a recharge mid-day.
  • The hidden 360 Wh: keeping the AC outlets on for a day costs about 15 W × 24 h of inverter draw, more than the router, bulbs and phones combined.
  • Leave the full freezer closed. It holds about 48 hours (24 if half full) per FoodSafety.gov, so it doesn't need battery in a one-day outage. The fridge does: it holds only about 4 hours.

How long outages actually last

Most outages are short. The EIA's utility reliability data put the average U.S. customer at 262.6 minutes without power in 2025 across 1.34 interruptions, about 3.3 hours per interruption on average (EIA Electric Power Annual, Table 11.3). Storm years are different: in 2024 the average customer lost 611.3 minutes, the most in a decade, and the EIA says major events such as Hurricanes Beryl, Helene and Milton caused about 80% of those hours (EIA Today in Energy).

So plan in two layers. A UPS-style station handles the routine 3-hour outage without you noticing. The 24-hour plan is for the storm that takes out a neighborhood, which is exactly when a fridge and a medical device matter. If you expect longer, go to the 3-day outage plan, which adds solar and generator recharge.

Read it this way: if your tier shows "24 h ✓", one charge gets you through the day with no recharge. If it shows hours, that is when the battery runs out. For a fridge, anything above about 20 hours is workable because the fridge holds about 4 hours on its own with the door shut, so you can let it coast for the last stretch. That is a food-safety margin, not a plan for medicine; see the insulin fridge guide for that.

Fridge, freezer and food: what the agencies say

  • Fridge: about 4 hours. FoodSafety.gov (citing USDA) says the refrigerator keeps food safe for up to 4 hours in an outage if you keep the door closed; discard meat, poultry, fish, eggs and leftovers after 4 hours without power (FoodSafety.gov).
  • Freezer: about 48 hours full, 24 hours half full, with the door shut. Food with ice crystals or at 40°F or below can be refrozen. In a one-day outage, a full standalone freezer doesn't need battery at all; spend the watt-hours on the fridge.
  • Fridge-freezer combos are one appliance. You can't power the fridge half and skip the freezer half. Keep the whole unit on the station and the doors shut.
  • Medical supplies: Ready.gov tells people who rely on refrigerated medicines or powered medical devices to plan with their doctor before an outage (Ready.gov). Insulin has its own room-temperature rules; see the insulin fridge guide.
  • Generators outside only. If a 24-hour outage turns into a generator night, the CDC's carbon monoxide guidance applies: outdoors, at least 20 feet from windows and doors (CDC).

Stretch one charge through the day

  1. Charge to 100% when the storm warning goes out. If you set a charge limit below 100% in the station's app for battery life, raise it the day before.
  2. Keep the fridge closed and cold. Every door opening costs compressor time. Plan meals so you open it a few times a day.
  3. Turn the AC outlets off when nothing needs them (no fridge, or the fridge has been unplugged for its 4-hour window). The inverter idle is about 15 W whether you use it or not.
  4. Run the CPAP on DC with the maker's converter or cord; a humidifier-off AirSense 11 uses about 7 W on DC versus about 22 W through an inverter once idle is counted (ResMed power table).
  5. Use USB-C for phones, laptops and lamps. USB ports run without the inverter.
  6. Skip heat and cooking. A 1,500 W space heater empties a 2 kWh station in a little over an hour (see the space heater guide); microwaves and kettles are fine in short bursts on a 1,800 W-plus unit but cost real Wh.
  7. Check the app at bedtime. If the fridge is going to run the battery out overnight, unplug the fridge at night (it holds about 4 hours) and keep the CPAP running.

A 24-hour runbook

  1. Storm warning: charge the station to 100%; freeze water bottles to fill empty freezer space; put an appliance thermometer in the fridge.
  2. Hour 0: plug the fridge and router into the station's AC outlets; CPAP on DC; phones on USB. Note the battery %.
  3. Hours 1–12: keep the fridge closed. Check the station app: on a 2 kWh unit with a top-freezer fridge you should be near 50% at hour 12.
  4. Bedtime: if you are behind, unplug the fridge for up to 4 hours overnight and keep the CPAP running.
  5. Hour 24: if the power isn't back, switch to the 3-day plan: recharge from a generator outdoors or solar, and decide what to give up.

Questions

What size power station do I need for a 24-hour power outage?

For lights, Wi-Fi, phones and a humidifier-off CPAP, a 1 kWh station (about 745 Wh needed). With a full-size top-freezer fridge, a 2 kWh station (about 1,739 Wh). A bottom-freezer or French-door fridge needs about 2,218 Wh, a bit more than a 2 kWh station delivers, so plan on letting the fridge coast for its 4-hour window or recharging once.

Can a 1,000 Wh power station run a refrigerator for 24 hours?

Not a full-size one. A 1,024 Wh station runs a median ENERGY STAR top-freezer fridge about 15 hours and a bottom-freezer about 11 hours, counting about 15 W of inverter idle. A compact or dorm fridge is different; see the original mix on this page.

Do I need to power my freezer in a 24-hour outage?

Usually not. FoodSafety.gov says a full freezer holds a safe temperature for about 48 hours, or 24 hours if half full, with the door closed. The refrigerator only holds about 4 hours, so spend battery on the fridge.

How long do power outages usually last?

The EIA's 2025 data show the average U.S. customer had 1.34 interruptions totaling 262.6 minutes, about 3.3 hours per interruption. Storm years are much worse: in 2024 the average was 611.3 minutes, and major hurricanes caused about 80% of those hours.

Why does inverter idle matter for a 24-hour outage?

A station's inverter draws power just by being on, about 15 W on many units. Over 24 hours that is about 360 Wh, more than a router, two LED bulbs and two phone charges combined. A fridge keeps the AC outlets on all day, so the idle is part of the budget.

Is a 2,000 Wh power station enough for a fridge and a CPAP for a day?

For a top-freezer fridge and a humidifier-off CPAP on DC, yes, just: about 1,739 Wh against about 1,740 Wh usable. With a bottom-freezer fridge, a humidified CPAP on AC and a TV, no; that is about 2,918 Wh.

Why can a station cover the watt-hours and still be a surge risk?

Stored energy and startup are different tests. A pack can hold the day's average and still fail to start the compressor. We only clear stations with a published motor surge figure for fridges.
