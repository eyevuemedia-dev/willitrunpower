Anker SOLIX F3800 vs F3800 Plus: what changed, what it runs, and who should buy one

Anker's F3800 product link now redirects to the F3800 Plus, listed at $2,999.99 and marked Sold Out on Anker's US store on November 11, 2026. Both hold 3,840 Wh of LiFePO4 and put out 6,000 W at 120/240 V. The Plus adds a 240 V generator bypass and 165 V solar inputs, and trades the original's NEMA 14-50 outlet for an RV-style TT-30. Buy one only if you need 240 V (well pump, transfer switch, EV, dryer) or multi-day backup. For 120 V essentials, a 2 kWh station at about a third of the price does the job.

Short answer

  • Same battery, same inverter rating. 3,840 Wh LFP (3,000+ cycles), 6,000 W total AC, 120/240 V split-phase on the L14-30R. Each bank of three NEMA 5-20R outlets is limited to 2,400 W (120 V / 20 A).
  • The Plus fixes the generator story. It accepts a 240 V or 120 V pure-sine generator (THD under 5%) through its Home Power Panel port and passes up to 6,000 W at 240 V. The original only takes a 120 V generator as a grid substitute.
  • The Plus solar inputs take long strings. 2 × 1,600 W at 11–165 V (17 A) versus 2 × 1,200 W at 11–60 V on the original.
  • Idle draw is high. Anker lists about 40 W with AC on and nothing plugged in (about 95 hours to empty). That cuts small-load runtimes by a quarter to a half.
  • Plugged into a 120 V wall outlet, the 240 V outlet is off. Only the three UPS outlets and the TT-30R work, with a 1,440 W bypass limit.
  • Our pick: F3800 Plus when it is back in stock if you need 240 V plus a generator plan. A leftover original F3800 only makes sense at a clear discount and if you will never run a 240 V generator through it.

The 40 W idle problem: runtimes at Willrun's 15 W and Anker's 40 W

Willrun normally adds about 15 W of inverter draw to every AC runtime. A 6,000 W split-phase inverter burns more than that. Anker's own FAQ for the F3800 Plus says it uses about 40 W with AC on and no devices connected. For a 1,500 W heater that barely matters. For a CPAP or a router it is the biggest load in the room. Formula: usable Wh ÷ (load W + idle W), with 3,264 Wh usable on one unit and 6,528 Wh with one BP3800.

Appliance watts are the published or ENERGY STAR-derived averages in Willrun's appliance records. Compressor loads cycle, so real fridge runtimes swing with door openings and room temperature. The 40 W figure is Anker's for the F3800 Plus; we have not found a published idle figure for the original F3800 and assume it is similar.

How to claw it back: run small DC loads from the 12 V car output or USB-C instead of the inverter (a CPAP with a maker DC cord, a router on a USB-C or 12 V adapter), and switch AC off whenever nothing on it needs power (overnight with only DC loads, for example). Every hour the inverter is off saves about 40 Wh.

Which outlets work when (F3800 Plus)

This is the part of the manual most buyers miss. Which outlets are live depends on what is plugged into the station:

Input connectedLive outletsLimit and switchover
Nothing (battery only)All: TT-30R, L14-30R (120/240 V), six NEMA 5-20R6,000 W total; 2,400 W per 5-20R bank
120 V wall outletThree left NEMA 5-20R (UPS) and the TT-30R (no UPS)1,440 W bypass; 20 ms switchover on the UPS outlets. L14-30R and right-hand 5-20R are off
120 V generator (via HPP port)TT-30R only, and only on the L1 phase3,000 W bypass; 30 ms to generator, 50 ms back to battery
240 V generator (via HPP port)TT-30R and L14-30R3,000 W (TT-30R) and 6,000 W (L14-30R) bypass; the 5-20R outlets are off

Source: Anker F3800 Plus FAQ Q5, Q10, Q11 and Q13, checked 2026-11-11.

Two consequences. First, a 20 ms switchover is fine for routers, TVs and most chargers, but it is not a true online UPS: Intel's ATX power supply guide only calls for about 16–17 ms of hold-up at full load, so a busy desktop PC can reboot on the switch. Second, if you keep the station on a wall outlet as a "UPS" for your house through an inlet, your 240 V circuits are not backed up while it charges. Unplug the wall charge, or feed it from solar, before you need 240 V.

240 V loads: well pumps, transfer switches and what 6,000 W really covers

  • Well pumps. Divide surge watts by 240 V to compare with the motor's locked-rotor amps. On the original's 9,000 W, that is 37.5 A: it clears a 1/2 HP 3-wire 4-inch motor (23 A), is thin on a 3/4 HP 3-wire (34.2 A), and is short for 1 HP 2-wire (48.7 A) in Franklin's tables. Full method in the well pump power station guide.
  • Central air. Running watts are roughly the condenser nameplate RLA × 240 V plus the air handler, which leaves room under 6,000 W for many 2–3 ton systems. Starting one is the problem, so plan on a soft-start kit and check the condenser's LRA against 37.5 A. We have no published Anker test to cite, so treat it as unverified.
  • Dryers, ranges, EVs. A 24 A Level 2 EV session at 240 V is about 5,760 W, so one unit's 3,264 usable Wh adds roughly 10–13 miles of range at 3–4 miles per kWh. The station is a poor EV charger and a fine emergency one.
  • Wiring. Feed house circuits only through a listed inlet box with an interlock, or a transfer switch (or Anker's Home Power Panel), installed to code. Never backfeed through a dryer outlet. The house generator size guide covers load lists.

Sizing check: the fridge + freezer + router + CPAP row in the idle table averages about 157 W, which is about 16 hours on one unit at 40 W idle and about 33 hours with one BP3800, before any furnace blower, well pump or lighting. Multi-day outages need solar or a generator, which is where the Plus's 240 V generator bypass earns its keep. See the 3-day outage plan.

Solar wiring: why 165 V matters

Open-circuit voltage (Voc) adds up in series and rises in cold weather, so you need headroom below the input limit. With a hypothetical rooftop-style panel at 45 V Voc:

Input limitPanels in series (45 V Voc, ~20% cold margin)Per-port power capWhat that means
Original F3800: 60 V, 1,200 W1 (2 × 45 V = 90 V is over the limit)1,200 WYou need low-voltage portable panels or many parallel strings
F3800 Plus: 165 V, 17 A, 1,600 W3 (135 V; cold-weather Voc stays under 165 V)1,600 WThree 400–500 W panels per port, wired in series, with fewer cables

Example panel only: check the Voc and temperature coefficient on your panel's label. Anker warns that exceeding 165 V can damage the solar ports. Anker also says the Plus cannot recharge from rooftop solar through the Home Power Panel during an outage; wire panels to the station's own solar ports.

As a rule of thumb, daily harvest is panel watts × peak sun hours × about 0.8: roughly 4–5 kWh from 1,200 W of panels in a sunny month, far less in winter. For your city, use SolarSized's sizing guides.

Expansion batteries

Each F3800 or F3800 Plus takes up to six BP3800 packs (3,840 Wh and 72.8 lb each), for 26,880 Wh on one unit. Anker's marketing figure of 53.8 kWh and 12 kW is two units on a Home Power Panel or Double Power Hub. Anker notes that the station and packs slowly equalize their charge levels. One BP3800 doubles every runtime in the table above. For whole-home storage sizing, see SolarSized's battery storage sizing guide.

Who should buy which

  • Buy the F3800 Plus (when Anker restocks) if you have a 240 V well pump up to 3/4 HP 3-wire, a transfer switch or inlet you want to feed at 240 V, or a plan to pair a 240 V generator. Unlike the original F3800, it can pass a 240 V generator through to its L14-30R outlet.
  • Consider a leftover original F3800 only at a clear discount to the Plus, and only if you never plan to run a generator through it, use portable 60 V-class solar, or need its NEMA 14-50 outlet.
  • Skip both if everything you need is 120 V and under 2,000 W. A DELTA 2 Max or Explorer 2000 v2 costs about a third as much and weighs a third as much. See the refrigerator outage guide and the home backup guide.
  • Look at the DELTA Pro 3 if 4,000 W is enough and you want a lighter unit (also currently unavailable at EcoFlow's store).

Questions

What is the difference between the Anker SOLIX F3800 and F3800 Plus?

Same 3,840 Wh LFP battery and 6,000 W 120/240 V inverter. The Plus accepts a 240 V or 120 V generator through its Home Power Panel port (up to 6,000 W bypass at 240 V), has 165 V solar inputs at 1,600 W each instead of 60 V at 1,200 W, and swaps the 14-50R outlet for a TT-30R. It is about 4 lb heavier.

Is the Anker F3800 discontinued?

Anker's F3800 product URL now redirects to the F3800 Plus page, which listed the Plus at $2,999.99 and Sold Out on November 11, 2026. Remaining original F3800 units may still appear at other retailers.

How long will the F3800 run a CPAP?

At the ResMed AirSense 11 user-guide typical of 56.1 W, with 3,264 usable Wh and Anker's stated 40 W AC idle, about 34 hours on AC (about 46 hours at Willrun's usual 15 W idle). The Plus spec list includes a 12 V, 10 A car output, so a maker-approved CPAP DC cord avoids most of the idle loss. Confirm any medical backup plan with your doctor or equipment supplier.

Can I use the 240 V outlet while the F3800 Plus is charging?

Not from a 120 V wall outlet. Anker says that while it recharges on 120 V AC, only the three UPS 120 V outlets work, plus the TT-30R without UPS, with a 1,440 W bypass limit. With a 240 V generator on the Home Power Panel port, the L14-30R and TT-30R work.

Can the F3800 run a well pump?

On paper, a 1/2 HP 3-wire 4-inch submersible (23 A locked-rotor) clears the original F3800's 9,000 W surge, which works out to 37.5 A at 240 V. A 3/4 HP 3-wire (34.2 A) has a thin margin, and 1 HP 2-wire (48.7 A) does not clear. Anker's F3800 Plus spec list does not publish a surge figure.

What is the F3800 Plus idle power draw?

About 40 W with AC on and nothing connected, according to Anker's FAQ, or roughly 95 hours to drain a full unit. Turn AC off when you do not need it and run small loads from DC.

What does the 0.85 factor do?

Willrun multiplies nameplate watt-hours by 0.85 before dividing by average draw. That allowance is for inverter loss. It is not a measured efficiency for this model.
